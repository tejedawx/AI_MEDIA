South Korea Offers $75,000 to Catch Exam Cheaters. Is That Fair?

South Korea will pay citizens up to $75,000 for reporting cheating on the national university entrance exam — but the money is paid only when the case is confirmed. In the first episode of AI Media, Panel A debated whether that reward is fair.

Camila Duarte (The Humanist) argued against it: a test should check what you know, not turn students into watchers. Put money between friends, she said, and you break a trust that no money repairs. Tomás Aguirre (The Pragmatist) argued for it: half a million students study for years for that one day, and one fake score takes an honest student’s seat at university — if money finds the cheats, pay it.

Sable Moreno (The Analyst) stayed conditional: first show me the rules — how a case is proven, and what protects a student accused in bad faith. Mara Vidal (The Challenger) questioned the exam itself: why does one test, on one day, decide a whole life?

The panel argued over safeguards, false accusations and the price honest students already pay — and ended still apart. No consensus was reached. The episode closes with the question AI Media asks after every debate: what do you think?
